The `refract` tier models a real piece of glass resting on the page: flat on the bottom, flat on
top, with a curved rim, the **bezel**, where the two meet. Light passing through the flat middle
goes straight down. Light hitting the bezel bends, so what you see near the edge comes from
further inside, squeezed toward the rim.

## The model

The glass is `thickness` px tall. Its curved rim rises over the bezel width; whatever thickness is
left over is a flat slab underneath. For a point at distance `s` across the bezel (0 at the rim, 1
where the glass turns flat):

1. **Surface height** follows a convex squircle, which joins the flat top without a visible ring:
   `h(s) = rise · (1 − (1 − s)⁴)^¼`, with `rise = min(thickness, bezel)`.
2. **Incidence:** a vertical view ray meets the surface at angle `θ₁ = atan(h′(s))` from its normal.
3. **Snell's law:** inside the glass it travels at `θ₂ = asin(sin θ₁ / n)`, where `n` is the
   index of refraction.
4. **Displacement:** the ray now leans by `θ₁ − θ₂` and crosses the rest of the glass,
   `slab + h(s)`, before reaching the page, so it lands `(slab + h(s)) · tan(θ₁ − θ₂)` px further
   in, toward the center.

Bending is strongest near the rim and fades to zero where the glass turns flat. In the corners the
same profile runs along the distance to the rounded rectangle, bending radially.

### The whole surface is a lens

With only a curved rim, the flat middle of the glass shows the backdrop unchanged, and the material
reads as glass only at its edges. Two options carry it across the whole component, the way
[ybouane/liquidglass](https://github.com/ybouane/liquidglass) does:

- **`magnify`** (default `0.1`) samples the whole surface toward its center, so everything behind
  it looks `1 / (1 − magnify)` times larger, like through a magnifier.
- **A bezel as wide as half the smaller side** curves the entire surface. With **`curvature: 2`**
  the profile is a circle: a pillow of glass rather than a slab with rounded edges.

Pick **Lens (ybouane-like)** in the demo above to see both together.

### Keeping the image in one piece

Pure optics overshoots in a thin band at the rim: the shift falls off so fast that neighboring
pixels sample past each other and the image folds over itself (a caustic). glass-midas caps the
local magnification at 5×. Where the physical curve is within that limit it's used as is; in the
caustic band it becomes a smooth, strongly compressed edge, which is what real glass looks like at
UI scale. Magnification counts toward that limit, so the rim gets less room the more the surface
magnifies.

### Caustics: the side wall

Thick glass with a round edge does something the guard prevents: near the rim, light bends so hard
that the image folds over itself into a compressed, mirrored band of the backdrop. That band reads
as the **side wall of the glass**, and it's what gives a thick lens its 3D look.

Turn it on with `caustics: true` and give the glass real thickness, for example
`{ thickness: 160, bezel: 60, curvature: 2, caustics: true, blur: '0px' }`, the **Lens** look in
the demo. Keep blur low: frost is applied before the bend and smears the band away.

## From optics to pixels

`displacementMap()` evaluates the model across the bezel once, then looks it up for every pixel and
stores the result in an image: red for horizontal shift, green for vertical, with 128 meaning
none. The strongest shift is stretched to the full 8-bit range: under a tenth of a pixel per step
for thin glass, but over a pixel for thick glass with caustics, which can show as faint banding.
The map is drawn at up to 2× the CSS size on high-density screens.

The browser does the rest on the GPU:

```
backdrop ──► blur ──► saturate ──► feDisplacementMap (map) ──► 0.6px smoothing ──► + rim highlight
```

Frost comes first so the highlight added at the end stays sharp. The light smoothing hides the
blocky look of magnified pixels in the compressed rim.

The blue channel carries the **rim highlight**, shaped by Fresnel reflectance: glass reflects 4%
of light head-on but most of it at grazing angles, so only the steepest part of the rim lights up
as a thin line. It's brightest where the rim faces a light at the top left.

## Options

| Option      | Default                   | Effect                                                                               |
| ----------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `thickness` | `24`                      | Total height of the glass in px. Thicker bends more. `0` turns bending off.          |
| `ior`       | `1.5`                     | Index of refraction: water `1.33`, glass `1.5`, sapphire `1.77`, diamond `2.42`.     |
| `bezel`     | `20`                      | Width of the curved rim in px. Narrower rims look harder and more liquid.            |
| `radius`    | `18`                      | Corner radius in px. Match the element's `border-radius`, or the corners bend wrong. |
| `specular`  | `0.6`                     | Rim highlight strength, 0 to 1.                                                      |
| `blur`      | `var(--gm-blur-md, 16px)` | Frost applied after bending. Lower values show more of the refraction.               |

```ts
const glass = createGlass(el, { radius: 28, thickness: 32, ior: 1.5 });
glass.update({ ior: 1.33 }); // turn it into water
```

## Cost

The map is computed on the CPU when the surface is created and whenever its size or optics change.
**Moving it costs nothing extra**: the map depends only on size, so dragging or scrolling glass
just moves the filter with the element. Avoid animating its size or optics every frame.
